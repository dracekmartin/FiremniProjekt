\section{Introduction}

The author works for M-line software as a software engineer, primarily on the EDISON product line, integrated into the team's standard development practices. The ``academic team'' for the project is this M-line development team. M-line is a Czech software company developing the EDISON information system for the transportation and logistics domain (public transport, payroll, timetables, and many other related agendas) on top of an InterSystems Caché backend with a Windows desktop client. The company plans an incremental modernization of the legacy .NET Framework + WinForms client onto a more modern stack of .NET Core + WPF.

The core goal of the project is to establish modern processes and an architectural foundation that subsequent in-house work will reuse. A concrete software piece -- a modernized client shell -- serves as both a working product and as a demonstration of the processes and patterns being introduced.

\medskip
\noindent The expected outcomes, as committed to in the approved project proposal. A few criteria shifted since approval: the pilot criterion was tightened, the testing commitment was narrowed to testable logic, and the reuse preparation of the licensing model became a nice to have.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Testing infrastructure} on both the .NET and the Caché side, establishing a test-first culture absent from the legacy stack.
  \item \textbf{Architectural foundation and reusable toolkit} for future agenda implementations, including a backward-compatibility layer that hosts legacy agendas.
  \item \textbf{Licensing modernization}: a new licensing data model and store, migration of the existing licenses into it, migration of the module tree definitions from XML files into the Caché backend, and the related internal tooling created or rewritten.
  \item \textbf{Update mechanism modernization} on both the client and the server side.
  \item \textbf{Modernized shell with a mini-app subsystem} and at least one demonstrative mini-app.
  \item \textbf{Pilot deployment} on developer workstations and at least one customer site switched fully to the new shell.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Section~\ref{sec:description} breaks these down into concrete requirements.

\subsection{Relation to other projects}
\label{sec:relation}

The project is being developed alongside several existing M-line projects with which it must coexist:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Legacy EDISON} (VB/C\# .NET Framework WinForms) -- the production system currently running at customer sites. The modernized version will run against the same Caché backend, share the general idea of responsibilities and will incrementally take over selected agendas. No breaking changes to the legacy contract are permitted during the transition.
  \item \textbf{InterSystems Caché backend} -- the shared data store. It is used by most of the company's projects. Many of the project's architectural decisions will be influenced by the fact that our deployment process is much faster when deploying just a Caché update (and not the .NET). This is impossible to change, due to the non-negotiable compatibility with the old agendas. A concrete example of this is the licensing. However, many lower-level decisions, like those in the toolkit for developers, will also be influenced by this fact.
  \item \textbf{TemplateDesigner} -- a WPF agenda concerning documents (printable timetables, invoices, etc.), already written by a colleague partially in the new stack. It serves as a second consumer validating the toolkit design (alongside the shell itself); its needs will influence which parts of the toolkit are prioritized.
\end{itemize}

\section{Project description}
\label{sec:description}

\textbf{End users and functionality.} EDISON installations are operated primarily by Czech transportation and logistics companies, primarily public-transport providers. Daily users are back-office staff in roles such as dispatching, payroll administration, ticketing, and accounting. The system covers the operational lifecycle of a transport company, with currently around forty agendas corresponding to specific business processes (fleet operations, scheduling, payroll, ticketing, asset management, subsidies, accounting, reporting, etc.).

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{Stakeholders and value.} The primary stakeholder is M-line software, the company developing and maintaining EDISON; the resulting foundation will be reused by in-house developers to migrate further agendas after the project ends. The secondary stakeholders are the customer organizations operating EDISON installations, who will benefit from the overall improvements in the development process (speed, testing) and will receive a more modern user experience.

\medskip
\noindent The new foundation and toolkit will make (re)implementing new agendas easier in a number of ways:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Testability reduces manual regression checking and protects against breaks during refactoring.
    \item The architecture will (at least partially) enforce testability and responsibility separation. This separation is especially murky in parts of the old codebase, which makes modifications to it very difficult.
    \item Mini-apps let agendas surface key state and tasks directly in the shell.
    \item The new stack will be much more supportive of AI coding assistants, both by the nature of the technologies used (WinForms UI definitions are a notorious problem) and the mentioned testability and architecture benefits.
\end{itemize}

Other than the benefits for the new agendas, the previously mentioned points that generally fall under the umbrella of the shell will be improved and will be rid of a number of pain points (which slow down development):
\begin{itemize}
    \item Modernization of licensing will allow us to extend the system for new features. But even the old functionalities will be made more user-friendly, with the users being:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Developers, when they need to add or modify the relationships between modules that they have developed.
        \item Licenser (company employee creating/modifying licenses), who will be able to use a more modern tool to express the constraints coming from agreements with end users more easily. The tool will be more in line with other tools the company has recently developed.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Update mechanism modernization will allow improvements, or at least prepare the systems for it, because the old one is simply too tangled to be modified. It has known security and reliability issues that nobody dares to fix, which makes this project the right opportunity to rewrite it.
    \item The old shell has a number of tangled issues with user (in)activity which are effectively impossible to solve, and negatively impact the systems of licensing, updating and the agendas themselves.
\end{itemize}

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{Mini-apps.} The legacy shell has only very limited means of showing agenda information outside the agendas themselves. The mini-app subsystem addresses this: an agenda can publish small at-a-glance views (summaries, reminders, key tasks) that the shell displays without the user opening the full agenda. The feature targets users who work across multiple agendas during the day and need a quick overview of their state. Mini-apps are opt-in per user: each user chooses in their settings which mini-apps to display. It is not a global or per-deployment choice.

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{AI-augmented development.} The project is developed with extensive use of AI coding assistants throughout. Rather than treating AI tooling as a passive convenience, the project will explicitly document the patterns and guardrails that make it productive: project memory in the form of in-repo guidance files, verification pipelines before accepting generated changes, and a preference for explicit and strongly typed dependency injection over hidden global states. This will increase the quality of the generated code and improve the whole process, including the code review.

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{Out of scope.} The project does not port existing agendas to the new stack. The demonstrative mini-app is written for an existing agenda, but the agenda itself is not rewritten. Adoption of the licensing data model by other M-line products is not part of the project (its product-agnostic design is requirement \req{L9}). The customer rollout ends with one pilot site switched to the new shell. The full rollout is a follow-up activity of the company. The project adds no new business functionality to the agendas themselves.

\medskip
The description below reflects the current plan. The project runs inside the company's everyday business, so priorities may shift as the work progresses. Significant deviations from this specification will be documented and justified in the final developer documentation.

\subsection{Functional requirements}
\label{sec:fr}

The requirements are grouped by project outcome and numbered for reference. Requirements marked \emph{(nice to have)} come last in each group and may be reduced under time pressure. The rest are essential.

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{Shell}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \reqdef{S1} The shell authenticates users against the Caché backend. The backend decides the authentication mode per user: a local EDISON account, a domain account, or the Windows identity without a password.
  \item \reqdef{S2} The shell establishes a company context at login (the last used company by default) and the user can switch companies at runtime, which closes open agenda windows first.
  \item \reqdef{S3} The shell presents a tree of available modules (agendas), filtered by the customer license and the user's permissions.
  \item \reqdef{S4} The module tree supports text search.
  \item \reqdef{S5} The user can mark favorite modules for quick access.
  \item \reqdef{S6} Every still-relevant legacy WinForms agenda can be launched through a compatibility bridge that passes the established session, company context, and the user's licensed permissions. The same holds for legacy windows that are not agendas, such as the data export and import used by developers, and for applications built on the new stack, which the shell launches with the same context.
  \item \reqdef{S7} Per-user settings (favorites, last company) persist and are restored on the next login; layouts persist per user and company.
  \item \reqdef{S8} The shell handles the session lifecycle cleanly: inactivity and forced logout with a user-visible countdown, detection of connection loss, and orderly shutdown. Closing the shell warns the user that all open agenda windows will close, and one action closes all open windows at once.
  \item \reqdef{S9} The shell verifies client/server version compatibility before and during a session. A mismatch at startup triggers the mandatory update (\req{U1}), a mid-session mismatch asks the user to restart into it, and the shell refuses to operate only when the update cannot proceed.
  \item \reqdef{S10} At first launch on an unconfigured workstation, the shell asks for the server connection, verifies it, and continues without a restart.
  \item \reqdef{S11} Users with backend-managed accounts can change their password in the shell, and the backend can require a password change at the next login.
  \item \reqdef{S12} The user can switch to a different user account at runtime, which closes open agenda windows first.
  \item \reqdef{S13} The shell offers a desktop area where the user arranges shortcut tiles for modules and launches them, persisted per user and company (the desktop is itself a mini-app living in the hosting area).
  \item \reqdef{S14} The shell lists the running agendas and can bring any of them to the front, and launching an agenda that already runs focuses the existing window instead of starting a second copy.
  \item \reqdef{S15} Where the backend allows it, the shell signs the user in under their Windows identity without showing the login dialog.
  \item \reqdef{S16} The login screen can remember the user name and, where the backend permits it, the password, stored encrypted on the workstation.
  \item \reqdef{S17} Session status information (user, company, client version, license expiry) is persistently visible in the main window.
  \item \reqdef{S18} \emph{(nice to have)} The user can mark one module as their startup module, and the shell opens it automatically after login.
  \item \reqdef{S19} \emph{(nice to have)} Next to the favorites, the shell keeps an automatically maintained list of the user's most used modules.
  \item \reqdef{S20} \emph{(nice to have)} Desktop shortcuts can start the shell and open a specific agenda directly.
  \item \reqdef{S21} \emph{(nice to have)} A brand-new server installation can be initialized with the new tooling: the company, the first administrator account, and the license import. Without this, the initialization stays a job for the legacy tools.
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{Mini-apps}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \reqdef{M1} The shell provides a hosting area where mini-apps can be arranged and resized, always remaining under shell management.
  \item \reqdef{M2} Agendas opt in by implementing a defined mini-app interface through which they publish their views instead of drawing into the shell directly.
  \item \reqdef{M3} Mini-apps are chosen per user: each user adds and removes them directly in the shell, and the choice persists per user and company. The one exception is the permanently present mini-app from \req{M6}.
  \item \reqdef{M4} A mini-app is offered only when its parent agenda is licensed and the user is permitted to use it.
  \item \reqdef{M5} At least one demonstrative mini-app working over real agenda data is delivered.
  \item \reqdef{M6} A permanently present mini-app shows company news and announcements (releases, training and similar), and the user cannot remove it. It is the company's first direct channel to the end users. The news is written by M-line staff on the company web, and the mini-app displays it from there.
  \item \reqdef{M7} The at-a-glance panels of the legacy shell remain available as mini-apps through their existing server contract, displayed with the toolkit's data list.
  \item \reqdef{M8} Open mini-apps can refresh their content automatically at a chosen interval.
  \item \reqdef{M9} Mini-apps respect the user's functional restrictions (the mechanism that narrows which data a user sees, distinct from permissions) when showing data, as the legacy panels do.
  \item \reqdef{M10} After a user or company switch, mini-apps reopen from the layout of the new context and never show data of the previous session. A damaged stored layout must not prevent the shell from starting.
  \item \reqdef{M11} The user's mini-app layout persists per user and company across sessions.
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{Licensing.} By licensing data we mean, per customer: the license itself (type and validity period), the purchased set of licensable features (modules and elements within them), and numeric limits (most importantly concurrent logins). The licenser edits licenses, developers define which elements belong to which licensable feature, and the shell consumes the result at login through the customer backend.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \reqdef{L1} Licensing data gets a new, structured data model and store in M-line's central archive, which becomes the authoritative source behind the existing delivery to customer backends. The delivery, including its automatic renewal before expiry, keeps emitting the format compatible with legacy clients. The shell keeps reading the license from the customer backend.
  \item \reqdef{L2} The shell consumes the license: it hides unlicensed modules, presents login rejections when the concurrent-user limit is exceeded, and shows non-blocking warnings for approaching limits and expiry. After the license expires, the installation stays read-only, as in the legacy system.
  \item \reqdef{L3} An editor for the licenser covers the common workflows (creating, modifying, and issuing customer licenses) and follows the conventions of the company's newer internal tools. Less common cases may stay in the legacy tool for a time.
  \item \reqdef{L4} The module tree definitions (which elements belong to which licensable feature, where a module sits in the tree) move from the XML files distributed with the client into the Caché backend. Developers maintain them as Caché code in the development environment, and the definitions reach customer installations with releases like any other code, replacing the manual XML maintenance. The license editor works over the catalog of licensable features derived from these definitions. The migration preserves the semantics of the existing definitions (hidden and internal-only entries, input parameters, window defaults), and the tools that read the XML keep working until they are replaced.
  \item \reqdef{L5} Existing licenses are migrated from the old store into the new data model with verified equivalence: a customer installation sees the same modules, limits, and validity before and after the migration.
  \item \reqdef{L6} When the concurrent-login limit is reached, an administrator can view the active sessions, force one out, and sign in.
  \item \reqdef{L7} An installation without a valid license still lets an administrator in far enough to import a new one, and administrator rights never bypass the license.
  \item \reqdef{L8} \emph{(nice to have)} The license can be refreshed at runtime without restarting the shell.
  \item \reqdef{L9} \emph{(nice to have)} The licensing data model is designed product-agnostically so other M-line products can adopt it later.
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{Update mechanism}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \reqdef{U1} At startup the client compares its version with the server installation and, on a mismatch, enforces a mandatory update before letting the user in. The download and apply steps may be carried out by the existing update engine. Replacing it is possible but not promised.
  \item \reqdef{U2} A runtime version guard rejects requests from stale clients, so client and server code never drift within a session. Server-only changes are tolerated by the version convention, as in the legacy system: a release marked as server-only does not force running clients out.
  \item \reqdef{U3} The server-side release distribution is modernized so that new and legacy clients are served from the same pipeline.
  \item \reqdef{U4} The new update mechanism coexists with the legacy one: legacy clients on the same installation keep updating unchanged.
  \item \reqdef{U5} The client can passively notify the user that a newer release exists without forcing installation.
  \item \reqdef{U6} After an update, the shell shows the user what changed in the release. Before opening an agenda whose package changed since the user last saw the notes, the shell shows what changed there, and the user can dismiss the notes until they change again. At the beginning the notes come from the legacy source: text files shipped with each release. The eventual goal is to write and serve them from the company web, with no per-release files traveling with the version.
  \item \reqdef{U7} The new distribution fixes two known failures of the legacy mechanism: it supports secure transport (HTTPS), and a Caché-only patch never forces running clients through the update cycle.
  \item \reqdef{U8} While the server side of an installation is being updated, new logins are refused. The administrator can force the running clients out, and the users get a countdown, about a minute, to save their work.
  \item \reqdef{U9} The company can pause the distribution of a release, and clients then behave as if no newer release existed.
  \item \reqdef{U10} On a server installation, only an authorized user can start the update, and the update does not start while other users work with the system.
  \item \reqdef{U11} \emph{(nice to have)} When the mandatory update cannot proceed, the user learns why in plain terms and the client exits in an orderly way.
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{Toolkit.} The toolkit's users are developers, so the requirements below describe what it must offer them.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \reqdef{T1} A view-model base with command creation, unified error presentation, and backend access, keeping view models free of UI-framework references.
  \item \reqdef{T2} Common controls: a server-driven data list with filtering and column persistence, input forms defined and validated by the backend (so a form change does not require redistributing clients), toolbars, standard dialogs, and a progress overlay.
  \item \reqdef{T3} Theming through shared design tokens, with a light and a dark theme.
  \item \reqdef{T4} A typed backend-access layer wrapping the Caché communication, with one shared session per client process.
  \item \reqdef{T5} A persistence service for per-user and per-company preferences.
  \item \reqdef{T6} A documented usage guide and test patterns for future agenda implementations, including runnable mock implementations of the backend-access layer and a test runner for the Caché side, so agenda logic is testable on both stacks without a live installation.
  \item \reqdef{T7} Data lists can export their contents, and printing is available where the legacy client offers it.
  \item \reqdef{T8} Windows remember their position and size per user and company, the user can reset all window layouts to their defaults, and a window with unsaved changes asks for confirmation before it closes. All of this comes from the toolkit, not from each window.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Non-Functional requirements}
\label{sec:nfr}

The same convention applies here: requirements marked \emph{(nice to have)} come last.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \reqdef{N1} Backward compatibility: no breaking changes to the legacy client-server contract, and the same Caché backend serves old and new clients simultaneously.
  \item \reqdef{N2} Parallel operation: the new and the legacy shell are installable and runnable side by side on one workstation and one installation.
  \item \reqdef{N3} Testability: all testable public logic of the toolkit and the shell (view models, services, non-visual components) is covered by unit tests on both stacks, and the tests run automatically before every push on the developer machines.
  \item \reqdef{N4} Extensibility: adding a new agenda requires only its registration, not changes to the shell. Building a first agenda screen with the toolkit should be a matter of days, not weeks.
  \item \reqdef{N5} Server-side configurability: behavior that changes per deployment or per customer (the module tree, licensing) lives in a database, not in the client binaries, so changing it does not require redistributing clients.
  \item \reqdef{N6} Performance: shell startup and agenda launch are not perceptibly slower than in the legacy shell, compared side by side on the same workstation.
  \item \reqdef{N7} Localization: the UI is Czech, and full multi-language support is not a goal of the project. What matters is that the toolkit centralizes the shared terms (OK, Cancel, Add, Edit, Delete and similar), so they are written once and stay consistent.
  \item \reqdef{N8} Theming: the light and dark themes are consistent across the shell, the toolkit, and mini-apps.
  \item \reqdef{N9} Platform: Windows 10/11 desktop, distributed through the company's existing release channel.
  \item \reqdef{N10} AI-assistant friendliness: in-repo guidance files, explicit and strongly typed dependency injection as the default for new code.
  \item \reqdef{N11} Security: licensing data in the new store is validated server-side, stored credentials are encrypted, and a failed update must leave the client startable. Full tamper-resistance of the license is explicitly out of reach while \req{N1} holds, because legacy clients must keep reading the compatible format.
  \item \reqdef{N12} Responsiveness: the UI stays responsive during backend work. Long operations run under a progress overlay instead of freezing the window.
  \item \reqdef{N13} Robustness: an unhandled error in one window does not terminate the client, and agendas launched by the shell never outlive it, so no orphaned process holds a license slot.
  \item \reqdef{N14} Coexistence of locks: record locks taken by one client are honored by the other, and a normal logout releases all locks of the session.
  \item \reqdef{N15} Keyboard: the shell and the toolkit components are operable by keyboard, though in complex windows the mouse may remain the easier way.
  \item \reqdef{N16} Error reporting: client errors are reported automatically to the company's support backend, and the user can add a description of what they were doing. A report can contain personal data (the user's identity, the content of a window), which is why the attached screenshots capture only the application's own windows, a deliberate narrowing against the legacy behavior. The user can also send a report on their own, without waiting for an error.
  \item \reqdef{N17} Usage telemetry: the client records which windows and controls are used and sends the aggregate to the backend, where it helps decide what to port next. The telemetry carries usage counts, not the content of the user's work.
  \item \reqdef{N18} Every non-trivial window offers a support panel with the company's support channels and version information that identifies the window's context.
  \item \reqdef{N19} The client upgrade preserves stored user preferences. If the stored state is unknown or damaged, the client falls back to defaults.
  \item \reqdef{N20} The inactivity limit is configured on the server, as in the legacy system, with an optional per-user override.
  \item \reqdef{N21} The user can switch the theme and adjust the application font at runtime, the new-stack UI scales with the font, and the choice is shared by the M-line applications on the workstation. Legacy WinForms agendas keep their own look.
  \item \reqdef{N22} \emph{(nice to have)} The shell can carry a company-specific color accent, as the legacy shell does, so that the user tells a production installation from a test one.
  \item \reqdef{N23} \emph{(nice to have)} An administrator can tell new-client sessions from legacy ones in the administration views.
  \item \reqdef{N24} \emph{(nice to have)} Administrative interventions (forced logouts, license and permission changes) leave an audit record.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

The solution consists of three cooperating parts, shown as containers in figure~\ref{fig:c4-container}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Modernized shell} -- the client application: login and session lifecycle, company selection, the module tree, launching agendas, and hosting mini-apps.
  \item \textbf{Shared toolkit} -- a library of reusable UI components, view-model infrastructure, theming, and the typed backend-access layer. Consumed by the shell, by TemplateDesigner, and by all future agenda implementations.
  \item \textbf{Legacy bridge} -- a backward-compatibility layer that hosts the existing WinForms agendas and passes them the established session and company context, so that the new shell can launch them without touching their source code. This is not a trivial layer: some agendas need specific exceptions in the host because of their age and the versions of standard components they use.
\end{itemize}

Agendas integrate with the shell through the mini-app interface: an agenda registers a factory for its mini-app view, and the shell instantiates the view, docks it, and manages its lifecycle and license checks. The shell never depends on concrete agendas. Each agenda only implements the interface the shell publishes.

The choice of WPF over WinForms matters especially for mini-apps and theming. Data binding, templating, and view composability make it natural to embed many small live views inside one window and to restyle them centrally. The legacy shell could only approximate this, which is why its ability to show agenda information has stayed so limited.
Two constraints shape the architecture throughout. First, no breaking changes to the legacy contract are permitted, which is why the legacy bridge exists as a separate layer and why both shells must keep working against the same backend during the transition. Second, deploying a database-side change is much faster than redistributing clients, so anything expected to change per deployment or per customer (the module tree, licensing data) belongs to a database rather than to the client. This is the direct motivation for requirements \req{L1} and \req{N5}. On the client, one shared backend session per shell process is a design rule: agendas and mini-apps hosted by the shell reuse the logical session established at login instead of opening their own.

The update work recreates the coordination logic: when a version mismatch is detected, how the mandatory update is enforced, and how it cooperates with the session lifecycle. The existing logic is too tangled to modify incrementally, and its behavior is entangled with the user-inactivity issues mentioned earlier. The low-level apply engine may be retained (\req{U1}). Update packages live as files on the customer installation's server, from where both shells update themselves. M-line delivers releases to customer servers through the existing channel, modernized so that one pipeline serves both clients (\req{U3}). The new mechanism must leave the legacy clients' update path untouched (\req{U4}).

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

The evaluation follows the three kinds of outcomes the project produces: code, a deployed product, and a toolkit for developers.

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{Automated testing.} All testable public logic of the toolkit and the shell ships with unit tests on both stacks, and the tests run automatically before every push (\req{N3}).

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{Pilot deployment.} The success criteria are directly measurable: all M-line developer workstations use the new shell as their default daily tool, at least one customer pilot site uses the new shell as its only shell, and all still-relevant legacy agendas remain launchable through the new shell. The pilot also exercises the licensing and update outcomes: the pilot installation runs on a license served from the new store (migrated with the equivalence check from \req{L5}) and its clients are updated through the new mechanism.

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{Developer study.} The toolkit's users are developers, so the user study takes place inside the company and runs continuously. The author gets feedback from the author of TemplateDesigner, as they use it, and shapes the toolkit accordingly. A deliberate goal of this loop is keeping the developer workflow close to the legacy one, so the switch costs the colleagues as little as possible. The toolkit is measured against the expectation stated in \req{N4}: a first agenda screen should be a matter of days, not weeks. Since porting agendas is out of scope, this is measured on the screens actually built with the toolkit during the project: the demonstrative mini-app and the TemplateDesigner screens adopting it.

\section{Risks}
\label{sec:risks}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Breaking the legacy contract.} A change on the shared backend could break legacy agendas at customer sites. Mitigation: the legacy bridge isolates the old code instead of modifying it, whether all legacy agendas remain launchable is checked continuously during development, and questions about legacy behavior go to the consultant, who knows that part of the stack best.
  \item \textbf{Update mechanism underestimated.} The legacy updater's behavior is tangled and partly undocumented, and the plan is a recreation of the coordination logic around it. Mitigation: the first step adapts the legacy updater to also serve the new shell, so a working fallback exists the whole time and the rest of the project does not block on the new mechanism.
  \item \textbf{Licensing migration underestimated.} The old license format is opaque and part of its semantics exists only in the code that reads and writes it. Mitigation: the migration is verified by the equivalence check from \req{L5}, and tests compare the encrypted license produced by the new tool byte by byte against the old one, not least because the cipher itself is aging. The data model comes before the editor. Fallback: the old store and its delivery keep running untouched until the equivalence is proven, so the migration can be re-run until it is right.
  \item \textbf{Problems at the pilot site.} The pilot does not depend on a customer volunteering: the company deploys the new shell to a selected site as part of a regular update, not as an opt-in, at least for a time. The real risk is an issue severe enough to send the site back to the legacy shell. Mitigation: months of daily internal use precede the pilot, and both shells stay installed side by side (\req{N2}), so a temporary return is possible.
  \item \textbf{Toolkit validated by only two consumers.} An API that fits the shell and TemplateDesigner may still not generalize to future agendas. Mitigation: the API is written to be extended, it is treated as provisional and versioned, design reviews are held with the TemplateDesigner author, and the feedback collected in the evaluation is used to correct the design.
  \item \textbf{AI-generated code quality.} Extensive use of AI assistants risks introducing many subtle defects. Mitigation: generated changes go through the same tests and code review as any other change, and the concrete rules that keep the assistants reliable (in-repo guidance files) are written down and become part of the AI documentation.
  \item \textbf{Scope and timeline pressure.} The licensing or update work may slip. Mitigation: the schedule puts the foundation first (testing, toolkit, shell), and each outcome can be reduced to a smaller but still useful version. For licensing, the data model and migration come before the editor.
  \item \textbf{Company priorities.} As an employee, the author can be pulled to urgent company work. Mitigation: the milestones are agreed with the company and progress is followed by the author's manager.
\end{itemize}

\section{Milestones / Deliverables}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{MS1 Testing infrastructure} -- test projects on both stacks wired into the development workflow.
  \item \textbf{MS2 Toolkit foundation} -- the core components, theming, and backend access, exercised by a first minimal version of the shell.
