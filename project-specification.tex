\section{Introduction}

The author works for M-line software as a software engineer, primarily on the EDISON product line, integrated into the team's standard development practices. The ``academic team'' for the project is this M-line development team. M-line is a Czech software company developing the EDISON information system for the transportation and logistics domain (public transport, payroll, timetables, and many other related agendas) on top of an InterSystems Caché backend with a Windows desktop client. The company plans an incremental modernization of the legacy .NET Framework + WinForms client onto a more modern stack of .NET Core + WPF.

The core goal of the project is to establish modern processes and an architectural foundation that subsequent in-house work will reuse. A concrete software piece -- a modernized client shell -- serves as both a working product and as a demonstration of the processes and patterns being introduced.

\medskip
\noindent The expected outcomes are those committed to in the approved project proposal. A few things shifted since the approval, most notably the licensing tool was taken out of the project. Section~\ref{sec:changes} lists the changes.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Testing infrastructure} on both the .NET and the Caché side, establishing a test-first culture absent from the legacy stack.
  \item \textbf{Architectural foundation and reusable toolkit} for future agenda implementations, including a backward-compatibility layer that hosts legacy agendas.
  \item \textbf{Licensing configuration}: the module tree definitions move from XML files into the Caché backend, and the shell keeps checking and respecting the license. The new licensing store and its editor were taken out of the project (section~\ref{sec:changes}).
  \item \textbf{Update mechanism modernization} on both the client and the server side.
  \item \textbf{Modernized shell with a mini-app subsystem} and at least one demonstrative mini-app.
  \item \textbf{Pilot deployment} on developer workstations and at least one customer site switched fully to the new shell.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Section~\ref{sec:description} breaks these down into concrete requirements.

\subsection{Relation to other projects}
\label{sec:relation}

The project is being developed alongside several existing M-line projects with which it must coexist:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Legacy EDISON} (VB/C\# .NET Framework WinForms) -- the production system currently running at customer sites. The modernized version will run against the same Caché backend, share the general idea of responsibilities and will incrementally take over selected agendas. No breaking changes to the legacy contract are permitted during the transition.
  \item \textbf{InterSystems Caché backend} -- the shared data store. It is used by most of the company's projects. Many of the project's architectural decisions will be influenced by the fact that our deployment process is much faster when deploying just a Caché update (and not the .NET). This is impossible to change, due to the non-negotiable compatibility with the old agendas. A concrete example of this is the licensing. However, many lower-level decisions, like those in the toolkit for developers, will also be influenced by this fact.
  \item \textbf{TemplateDesigner} -- a WPF agenda concerning documents (printable timetables, invoices, etc.), already written by a colleague partially in the new stack. It serves as a second consumer validating the toolkit design (alongside the shell itself); its needs will influence which parts of the toolkit are prioritized.
\end{itemize}

\section{Project description}
\label{sec:description}

\textbf{End users and functionality.} EDISON installations are operated primarily by Czech transportation and logistics companies, primarily public-transport providers. Daily users are back-office staff in roles such as dispatching, payroll administration, ticketing, and accounting. The system covers the operational lifecycle of a transport company through agendas corresponding to specific business processes (fleet operations, scheduling, payroll, ticketing, asset management, subsidies, accounting, reporting, etc.).

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{Stakeholders and value.} The primary stakeholder is M-line software, the company developing and maintaining EDISON; the resulting foundation will be reused by in-house developers to migrate further agendas after the project ends. The secondary stakeholders are the customer organizations operating EDISON installations, who will benefit from the overall improvements in the development process (speed, testing) and will receive a more modern user experience.

\medskip
\noindent The new foundation and toolkit will make (re)implementing new agendas easier in a number of ways:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Testability reduces manual regression checking and protects against breaks during refactoring.
    \item The architecture will (at least partially) enforce testability and responsibility separation. This separation is especially murky in parts of the old codebase, which makes modifications to it very difficult.
    \item Mini-apps let agendas surface key state and tasks directly in the shell.
    \item The new stack will be much more supportive of AI coding assistants, both by the nature of the technologies used (WinForms UI definitions are a notorious problem) and the mentioned testability and architecture benefits.
\end{itemize}

Other than the benefits for the new agendas, the previously mentioned points that generally fall under the umbrella of the shell will be improved and will be rid of a number of pain points (which slow down development):
\begin{itemize}
    \item Modernization of the licensing configuration gives developers a cleaner way to add or modify the relationships between modules that they have developed.
    \item Update mechanism modernization will allow improvements, or at least prepare the systems for it, because the old one is simply too tangled to be modified. It has known security and reliability issues that nobody dares to fix, which makes this project the right opportunity to rewrite it.
    \item The old shell has a number of tangled issues with user (in)activity which are effectively impossible to solve, and negatively impact the systems of licensing, updating and the agendas themselves.
\end{itemize}

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{Mini-apps.} The legacy shell has only very limited means of showing agenda information outside the agendas themselves. The mini-app subsystem fills this gap. An agenda can publish small views (summaries, reminders, key tasks) that the shell displays without the user opening the full agenda. The feature is meant for users who work in several agendas during the day and need a quick overview of their state. Each user chooses in their settings which mini-apps to display. The choice is per user, not global or per deployment.

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{AI-augmented development.} The project is developed with extensive use of AI coding assistants throughout. Rather than treating AI tooling as a passive convenience, the project will explicitly document the patterns and guardrails that make it productive: project memory in the form of in-repo guidance files, verification pipelines before accepting generated changes, and a preference for explicit and strongly typed dependency injection over hidden global states. This will increase the quality of the generated code and improve the whole process, including the code review.

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{Out of scope.} The project does not port existing agendas to the new stack. The demonstrative mini-app is written for an existing agenda, but the agenda itself is not rewritten. The new licensing store, its editor, and the migration of existing licenses are not part of the project (section~\ref{sec:changes}). The customer rollout ends with one pilot site switched to the new shell. The full rollout is the company's work after the project ends. The project adds no new business functionality to the agendas themselves.

\medskip
The description below reflects the current plan. The project runs inside the company's everyday business, so priorities may shift as the work progresses. Significant deviations from this specification will be documented and justified in the final developer documentation.

\subsection{Functional requirements}
\label{sec:fr}

The requirements are grouped by project outcome and numbered for reference. Requirements marked \emph{(nice to have)} come last in each group and may be reduced under time pressure. The rest are essential.

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{Shell}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \reqdef{S1} The shell authenticates users against the Caché backend. The backend decides the authentication mode per user: a local EDISON account, a domain account, or the Windows identity without a password.
  \item \reqdef{S2} The shell establishes a company context at login (the last used company by default) and the user can switch companies at runtime, which closes open agenda windows first.
  \item \reqdef{S3} The shell presents a tree of available modules (agendas), filtered by the customer license and the user's permissions.
  \item \reqdef{S4} The module tree supports text search.
  \item \reqdef{S5} The user can mark favorite modules for quick access.
  \item \reqdef{S6} Every still-relevant legacy WinForms agenda can be launched through a compatibility bridge that passes the established session, company context, the user's licensed permissions, and the license state (including the read-only mode after expiry). The same holds for legacy windows that are not agendas, such as the data export and import used by developers, and for applications built on the new stack, which the shell can launch with the same context.
  \item \reqdef{S7} Favorites and layouts persist per user and company, and the last used company per user. All of them are restored at the next login.
  \item \reqdef{S8} The shell handles the session lifecycle: inactivity and forced logout with a user-visible countdown, detection of connection loss, and orderly shutdown. Closing the shell warns the user that all open agenda windows will close, and one action closes all open windows at once.
  \item \reqdef{S9} The shell verifies client/server version compatibility before and during a session. A mismatch at startup triggers the mandatory update (\req{U1}), a mid-session mismatch asks the user to restart so that the update can run, and the shell refuses to operate only when the update cannot proceed.
  \item \reqdef{S10} At first launch on an unconfigured workstation, the shell asks for the server connection, verifies it, and continues without a restart.
  \item \reqdef{S11} Users with backend-managed accounts can change their password in the shell, and the backend can require a password change at the next login.
  \item \reqdef{S12} The user can switch to a different user account at runtime, which closes open agenda windows first.
  \item \reqdef{S13} The shell offers a desktop area where the user arranges shortcut tiles for modules and launches them, persisted per user and company (the desktop is itself a mini-app placed in the hosting area).
  \item \reqdef{S14} The shell lists the running agendas and can bring any of them to the front, and launching an agenda that already runs focuses the existing window instead of starting a second copy.
  \item \reqdef{S15} Where the backend allows it, the shell signs the user in under their Windows identity without showing the login dialog.
  \item \reqdef{S16} The login screen can remember the user name and, where the backend permits it, the password, stored encrypted on the workstation.
  \item \reqdef{S17} Session status information (user, company, client version, license expiry) is always visible in the main window.
  \item \reqdef{S18} The inactivity limit is configured on the server, as in the legacy system, with an optional per-user override.
  \item \reqdef{S19} \emph{(nice to have)} The user can mark one module as their startup module, and the shell opens it automatically after login.
  \item \reqdef{S20} \emph{(nice to have)} Next to the favorites, the shell keeps an automatically maintained list of the user's most used modules.
  \item \reqdef{S21} \emph{(nice to have)} Desktop shortcuts can start the shell and open a specific agenda directly.
  \item \reqdef{S22} \emph{(nice to have)} A new server installation can be initialized with the new tooling: the company, the first administrator account, and the license import. Without this, the initialization stays a job for the legacy tools.
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{Mini-apps}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \reqdef{M1} The shell provides a hosting area where mini-apps can be arranged and resized, always remaining under shell management.
  \item \reqdef{M2} Agendas opt in by implementing a defined mini-app interface through which they publish their views instead of drawing into the shell directly.
  \item \reqdef{M3} Mini-apps are chosen per user: each user adds and removes them in a settings window opened from the shell, and the choice persists per user and company. The one exception is the permanently visible news panel (\req{M6}).
  \item \reqdef{M4} A mini-app is offered only when its parent agenda is licensed and the user is permitted to use it.
  \item \reqdef{M5} At least one demonstrative mini-app working over real agenda data is delivered.
  \item \reqdef{M6} A permanently visible panel shows company news and announcements (releases, training and similar), and the user cannot remove it. Its content comes from the company's support portal, where M-line staff maintain it.
  \item \reqdef{M7} The overview panels of the legacy shell remain available as mini-apps through their existing server contract.
  \item \reqdef{M8} Open mini-apps can refresh their content automatically at a chosen interval. For the legacy panels this reloads the data prepared by a server task, and the recomputation stays on the server.
  \item \reqdef{M9} Mini-apps respect the user's functional restrictions (the mechanism that narrows which data a user sees, distinct from permissions) when showing data, as the legacy panels do.
  \item \reqdef{M10} After a user or company switch, mini-apps reopen from the layout of the new context and never show data of the previous session. A damaged stored layout must not prevent the shell from starting.
  \item \reqdef{M11} The user's mini-app layout persists per user and company across sessions.
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{Licensing.} By licensing data we mean, per customer: the license itself (type and validity period), the purchased set of licensable features (modules and elements within them), and numeric limits (most importantly concurrent logins). Developers define which elements belong to which licensable feature, and the shell respects the license at login through the customer backend.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \reqdef{L1} The shell respects the license: it hides unlicensed modules, shows the rejection of a login when the concurrent-user limit is exceeded, and shows non-blocking warnings for approaching expiry and, where the backend reports it, for approaching numeric limits. After the license expires, the installation stays read-only, as in the legacy system.
  \item \reqdef{L2} The module tree definitions (which elements belong to which licensable feature, where a module sits in the tree) move from the XML files distributed with the client into the Caché backend. Developers maintain them as Caché code in the development environment, and the definitions reach customer installations with releases like any other code, replacing the manual XML maintenance. The migration preserves the semantics of the existing definitions (hidden and internal-only entries, input parameters, window defaults), and the tools that read the XML keep working until they are replaced. During the transition one of the two forms is generated from the other, so that no definition is maintained twice.
  \item \reqdef{L3} When the concurrent-login limit is reached, an administrator can view the active sessions, force one out, and sign in.
  \item \reqdef{L4} An installation without a valid license still lets an administrator in far enough to import a new one, and administrator rights never bypass the license.
  \item \reqdef{L5} \emph{(nice to have)} The license can be refreshed at runtime without restarting the shell.
  \item \reqdef{L6} \emph{(nice to have)} An administrator can tell new-client sessions from legacy ones in the administration views.
  \item \reqdef{L7} \emph{(nice to have)} Administrative interventions (forced logouts, license and permission changes) leave an audit record.
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{Update mechanism}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \reqdef{U1} At startup the client compares its version with the server installation and, on a mismatch, enforces a mandatory update before letting the user in. The download and apply steps may be carried out by the existing update engine. Replacing it is possible but not promised.
  \item \reqdef{U2} A runtime version guard rejects requests from stale clients, so client and server code never drift within a session. Server-only changes are tolerated by the version convention, as in the legacy system: a release marked as server-only does not force running clients out.
  \item \reqdef{U3} The download of release files (packages, executables, libraries) to the customer server and from there to the workstations is modernized so that it serves both clients. Building and publishing a release stays as it is.
  \item \reqdef{U4} The new update mechanism coexists with the legacy one: legacy clients on the same installation keep updating unchanged.
  \item \reqdef{U5} The client can passively notify the user that a newer release exists without forcing installation.
  \item \reqdef{U6} After an update, the shell shows the user what changed in the release. Before opening an agenda whose package changed since the user last saw the notes, the shell shows what changed there, and the user can dismiss the notes until they change again. At the beginning the notes come from the legacy source: text files shipped with each release. The eventual goal is to write and serve them from the company web, with no per-release files traveling with the version.
  \item \reqdef{U7} The new download fixes two known failures of the legacy mechanism: it supports secure transport (HTTPS), and a Caché-only patch is picked up at the next start and never forces running clients out, unlike the legacy nightly update.
  \item \reqdef{U8} While the server side of an installation is being updated, new logins are refused. The administrator can force the running clients out, and the users get a countdown, about a minute, to save their work.
  \item \reqdef{U9} The company can pause the distribution of a release, and clients then behave as if no newer release existed.
  \item \reqdef{U10} On a server installation, only an authorized user can start the update, and the update does not start while other users work with the system.
  \item \reqdef{U11} \emph{(nice to have)} When the mandatory update cannot proceed, the user learns why in plain terms and the client exits in an orderly way.
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{Toolkit.} The toolkit's users are developers, so the requirements below describe what it must offer them.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \reqdef{T1} A view-model base with command creation, unified error presentation, and backend access, testable without a running UI.
  \item \reqdef{T2} Common controls: a server-driven data list with filtering and column persistence, input forms defined and validated by the backend (so a form change does not require redistributing clients), toolbars, standard dialogs, and a progress overlay.
  \item \reqdef{T3} Theming through shared theme resources (colors, fonts, spacing), with a light and a dark theme.
  \item \reqdef{T4} A typed backend-access layer wrapping the Caché communication, with one shared session per client process.
  \item \reqdef{T5} A persistence service for per-user and per-company preferences.
  \item \reqdef{T6} A documented usage guide and test patterns for future agenda implementations, including runnable mock implementations of the backend-access layer and a test runner for the Caché side, so agenda logic is testable on both stacks without a live installation.
  \item \reqdef{T7} Data lists can export their contents, and printing is available where the legacy client offers it.
  \item \reqdef{T8} Windows remember their position and size per user and company, the user can reset all window layouts to their defaults, and a window with unsaved changes asks for confirmation before it closes. All of this comes from the toolkit, not from each window.
  \item \reqdef{T9} Client errors are reported automatically to the company's support portal, and the user can add a description of what they were doing. A report can contain personal data (the user's identity, the content of a window), which is why the attached screenshots capture only the application's own windows, narrower than the legacy behavior. The user can also send a report on their own, without waiting for an error.
  \item \reqdef{T10} The client records which windows and controls are used and sends the usage events (window, control, time) to the backend, which aggregates them to help decide what to port next. The events carry no content of the user's work.
  \item \reqdef{T11} Every non-trivial window offers a support panel with the company's support channels and version information that identifies the window's context.
  \item \reqdef{T12} The client upgrade preserves stored user preferences. If the stored state is unknown or damaged, the client falls back to defaults.
  \item \reqdef{T13} The user can switch the theme and adjust the application font at runtime, the new-stack UI scales with the font, and the choice is shared by the M-line applications on the workstation. Legacy WinForms agendas keep their own look.
  \item \reqdef{T14} \emph{(nice to have)} The shell can carry a company-specific color accent, as the legacy shell does, so that the user can tell a production installation from a test one.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Non-Functional requirements}
\label{sec:nfr}

Each non-functional requirement states how it will be checked.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \reqdef{N1} Backward compatibility: no breaking changes to the legacy client-server contract, and the same Caché backend serves old and new clients simultaneously. Record locks taken by one client are honored by the other, and a normal logout releases all locks of the session. Checked on the pilot installation, where both shells run against one backend, and by the lock tests on the Caché side.
  \item \reqdef{N2} Parallel operation: the new and the legacy shell are installable and runnable side by side on one workstation and one installation. Checked on the developer workstations, which run this way for the whole project.
  \item \reqdef{N3} Testability: at least 80~\% of the non-visual types of the shell and the toolkit (view models, services, parsers) have unit tests, every Caché class with logic has a test class, and the tests run automatically before every push on the developer machines. Checked by counting the types and their tests in the repository.
  \item \reqdef{N4} Extensibility: adding an agenda built on the new stack requires only its registration, not changes to the shell. Checked on the demonstrative mini-app and on TemplateDesigner, which are added without touching the shell.
  \item \reqdef{N5} Server-side configurability: per-customer data (licensing, the inactivity limit) and shared definitions that must change without a client release (the module tree) live in the Caché backend, not in the client binaries. Checked by changing the inactivity limit and a module definition on a test installation without redistributing clients.
  \item \reqdef{N6} Performance: the shell starts faster than the legacy shell, and launching an agenda through the bridge takes at most twice as long as in the legacy shell, both measured side by side on the same workstation.
  \item \reqdef{N7} Localization: the UI is Czech, and full multi-language support is not a goal of the project. The toolkit keeps the shared terms (OK, Cancel, Add, Edit, Delete and similar) in one place, so they are written once and stay consistent. Checked by searching the shell and the toolkit for duplicated shared terms.
  \item \reqdef{N8} Theming: the light and dark themes are consistent across the shell, the toolkit, and mini-apps. Every window is walked through in both themes before the pilot.
  \item \reqdef{N9} Platform: Windows 10/11 desktop, distributed through the company's existing release channel. The pilot installation itself is the check.
  \item \reqdef{N10} Support for AI coding assistants: in-repo guidance files, and explicit, typed dependencies as the default for new code, with the toolkit's shared services reached through one documented place. Checked by reviewing new code against the guidance files.
  \item \reqdef{N11} Security: stored credentials are encrypted, and a failed update must leave the client startable. Full tamper-resistance of the license is out of reach while \req{N1} holds, because legacy clients must keep reading the compatible format. Checked by inspecting the stored credentials and by interrupting an update on a test workstation.
  \item \reqdef{N12} Responsiveness: the UI never freezes for more than two seconds without a progress indicator, and long operations run under a progress overlay. Checked during the daily internal use: a freeze noticed by the developers counts as a defect.
  \item \reqdef{N13} Robustness: an unhandled error in one window does not terminate the client, and agendas launched by the shell never outlive it, so no orphaned process keeps record locks or a stale session alive. Checked by raising an error in a test window and by killing the shell with agendas open.
  \item \reqdef{N14} Keyboard: the shell and the toolkit components are operable by keyboard, though in complex windows the mouse may remain the easier way. The check is a walk through login, the module tree, and launching an agenda without a mouse.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Mockups}

\subsection{Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

The solution consists of three cooperating parts, shown as containers in figure~\ref{fig:c4-container}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Modernized shell} -- the client application: login and session lifecycle, company selection, the module tree, launching agendas, and hosting mini-apps.
  \item \textbf{Shared toolkit} -- a library of reusable UI components, view-model infrastructure, theming, and the typed backend-access layer. Consumed by the shell, by TemplateDesigner, and by all future agenda implementations.
  \item \textbf{Legacy bridge} -- a backward-compatibility layer that hosts the existing WinForms agendas and passes them the established session and company context, so that the new shell can launch them without touching their source code. The layer is not trivial. Some agendas need specific exceptions in the host because of the way they manage their own windows and rights.
\end{itemize}

Agendas integrate with the shell through the mini-app interface. An agenda registers a factory for its mini-app view, and the shell instantiates the view, docks it, and manages its lifecycle and license checks. The shell never depends on concrete agendas. Each agenda only implements the interface the shell publishes.

The choice of WPF over WinForms matters especially for mini-apps and theming. Data binding, templating, and composable views make it easy to embed many small live views inside one window and to restyle them centrally. The legacy shell could only approximate this, which is why its ability to show agenda information has stayed so limited.

Two constraints shape the architecture. First, no breaking changes to the legacy contract are permitted, which is why the legacy bridge exists as a separate layer and why both shells must keep working against the same backend during the transition. Second, deploying a backend change is much faster than redistributing clients, so anything expected to change per deployment or per customer (the module tree, licensing data) belongs to the backend rather than to the client. Requirements \req{L2} and \req{N5} come from this. One more rule holds on the client: a shell process has one shared backend session, and the agendas and mini-apps it hosts reuse the logical session established at login instead of opening their own.

The update work recreates the coordination logic: when a version mismatch is detected, how the mandatory update is enforced, and how it cooperates with the session lifecycle. The existing logic is too tangled to modify incrementally, and its behavior is tied to the user-inactivity issues mentioned earlier. The low-level apply engine may stay (\req{U1}). Update packages are stored as files on the customer installation's server, from where both shells update themselves. The project modernizes the download of release files to the customer server and to the workstations (\req{U3}). How M-line builds and publishes a release stays as it is. The new mechanism must leave the legacy clients' update path untouched (\req{U4}).

\section{Platform and technologies}
\label{sec:platform}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Client}: C\# on \href{https://dotnet.microsoft.com/}{.NET} (a current LTS release), \href{https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/desktop/wpf/}{WPF}, the \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model-view-viewmodel}{MVVM} pattern.
  \item \textbf{Backend}: \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/InterSystems_Cach%C3%A9}{InterSystems Caché} and its \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cach%C3%A9_ObjectScript}{ObjectScript} language; communication over HTTP through the company's existing REST-style channel. The data channel stays inside the customer's network. The update download crosses the internet, which is why \req{U7} asks for HTTPS there.
  \item \textbf{Testing}: \href{https://xunit.net/}{xUnit} and \href{https://nsubstitute.github.io/}{NSubstitute} on .NET; the native \href{https://docs.intersystems.com/latest/csp/docbook/DocBook.UI.Page.cls?KEY=TUNT}{\%UnitTest} facilities on Caché.
  \item \textbf{Mini-app hosting}: an established WPF docking/layout library. The concrete choice is an implementation decision.
  \item \textbf{Development}: Git and GitHub with automated test execution hooked into the workflow; AI coding assistants with in-repo guidance files.
\end{itemize}

\noindent This is not a definitive list, and lower-level libraries will be chosen as the work progresses.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

The evaluation follows the three kinds of outcomes the project produces: code, a deployed product, and a toolkit for developers.

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{Automated testing.} At least 80~\% of the non-visual types of the shell and the toolkit have unit tests, every Caché class with logic has a test class, and the tests run automatically before every push (\req{N3}).

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{Pilot deployment.} Success means that all M-line developer workstations use the new shell as their default daily tool, that at least one customer pilot site uses the new shell as its only shell, and that all still-relevant legacy agendas remain launchable through the new shell. The pilot also covers the remaining outcomes: the installation runs on the module tree served from Caché (\req{L2}), the demonstrative mini-app (\req{M5}) is available there, and its clients are updated through the new mechanism.

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{Developer study.} The toolkit's users are developers, so the user study takes place inside the company and runs continuously. The author collects the TemplateDesigner author's feedback from using the toolkit and adjusts the toolkit accordingly. One goal of this feedback is to keep the developer workflow close to the legacy one where that does not conflict with the test-first approach, so the switch costs the colleagues as little as possible.

\section{Risks}
\label{sec:risks}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Breaking the legacy contract.} A change on the shared backend could break legacy agendas at customer sites. Mitigation: the legacy bridge isolates the old code instead of modifying it. Whether all legacy agendas remain launchable is checked continuously during development, and questions about legacy behavior go to the consultant, who knows that part of the stack best.
  \item \textbf{Update mechanism underestimated.} The legacy updater's behavior is tangled and partly undocumented, and the plan is to recreate the coordination logic around it. Mitigation: the first step adapts the legacy updater to also serve the new shell, so a working fallback exists the whole time and the rest of the project does not wait for the new mechanism.
  \item \textbf{Problems at the pilot site.} The pilot does not depend on a customer volunteering. The company deploys the new shell to a selected site as part of a regular update. The site is informed, but the switch is not optional for its users. The risk is an issue severe enough to send the site back to the legacy shell. Mitigation: months of daily internal use precede the pilot, and both shells stay installed side by side (\req{N2}), so a temporary return is possible.
  \item \textbf{Toolkit validated by only two consumers.} An API that fits the shell and TemplateDesigner may still not generalize to future agendas. Mitigation: the API is written to be extended and treated as provisional and versioned, the author holds design reviews with the TemplateDesigner author, and the design is corrected from the feedback collected in the evaluation.
  \item \textbf{AI-generated code quality.} Extensive use of AI assistants risks introducing many subtle defects. Mitigation: generated changes go through the same tests and code review as any other change, and the concrete rules that keep the assistants reliable (in-repo guidance files) are written down and become part of the AI documentation.
  \item \textbf{Scope and timeline pressure.} The update work or the mini-app subsystem may slip. Mitigation: the schedule puts the foundation first (testing, toolkit, shell), and each outcome can be reduced to a smaller but still useful version.
  \item \textbf{Company priorities.} As an employee, the author can be pulled away to urgent company work. Mitigation: the project is itself a company priority, so the company has its own interest in finishing it, and the other agendas in the author's care are in maintenance mode only. The milestones are agreed with the company, progress is followed by the author's manager, and any loss of capacity shows up in the next report to the supervisor.
  \item \textbf{Late-identified requirements.} Legacy behavior or user needs discovered late in the project can shift the assignment in small ways. Mitigation: small changes go into the regular reports to the supervisor, larger ones are discussed with him, and all of them are written up in the final documentation as justified deviations from this specification, as the faculty rules expect.
\end{itemize}

\section{Milestones / Deliverables}
\label{sec:milestones}

The work is split into six milestones, each ending with something to demonstrate:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{MS1 Testing infrastructure} -- test projects on both stacks wired into the development workflow.
  \item \textbf{MS2 Toolkit foundation} -- the core components, theming, and backend access, exercised by a first minimal version of the shell.
  \item \textbf{MS3 Shell prototype} -- login, the module tree fed from the module definitions moved into Caché (\req{L2}), and launching legacy agendas, all in daily use on developer machines.
  \item \textbf{MS4 Mini-app subsystem} -- the mini-app interface and hosting, with the demonstrative mini-app running over real data.
  \item \textbf{MS5 Update mechanism} -- the new client detects a version mismatch and updates itself from the customer server.
  \item \textbf{MS6 Pilot deployment} -- all developer workstations on the new shell by default and a customer pilot site using only the new shell.
\end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Developer documentation}: architectural overview, toolkit usage guide, and recommended development setup for future agenda implementations.
  \item \textbf{AI documentation}: patterns, guardrails, and observations collected during the project, delivered either as a standalone document or integrated with the developer documentation.
  \item \textbf{User documentation} for the new shell, written for end users and primarily highlighting new features and differences from the old shell.

\section{Changes against the proposal}
\label{sec:changes}

The proposal was approved in June 2026. This specification departs from it in a few places, all agreed with the supervisor.

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{The licensing tool was taken out.} The proposal promised a new licensing data model, a migration of the existing licenses into it, and a new tool for the licenser. This part turned out to be very isolated from the rest of the project. It is a separate web stack with its own data model, which the committee would have to review on top of the client work, and the remaining scope (shell, toolkit, mini-apps, update mechanism, pilot) is complex enough on its own. What stays is the part that touches the client: the shell keeps checking and respecting the license (\req{L1}), and the module tree definitions move from XML files into the Caché backend (\req{L2}). The preparation of the licensing model for reuse in other M-line products dropped out together with the tool.

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{The pilot criterion was tightened.} The proposal spoke of a customer site running the new shell in parallel with the legacy one. The pilot site now switches to the new shell as its only shell, so that its users really try it.

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{The update engine may stay.} The proposal expected the update mechanism to be most likely a full recreation. The project recreates the coordination around updates (detection, enforcement, cooperation with the session lifecycle) and modernizes the download of release files. The existing low-level engine that copies and applies the files may stay (\req{U1}).

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{One static entry point for shared services.} The proposal prefers explicit dependency injection over hidden global state. New code follows that, but the toolkit keeps one documented static entry point (a service locator) through which controls reach shared services such as theming, strings, and backend access, instead of a dependency injection container (\req{N10}).
